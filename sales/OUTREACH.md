# Kennedy's Inc. — Outreach

**Placeholders:** `[DEMO URL]`, `[YOUR NAME]`, `[YOUR PHONE]`, `[YOUR EMAIL]`, `[YOUR TOWN]`.

Two rules govern everything below.

1. Kennedy's did not ask for this, and you have never spoken to anyone there. Say so in the first three sentences of every message.
2. Write nothing about their current website. No criticism, no compliment, no mention at all.

---

## 1. Before you send the link

The demo keeps its data in whatever browser opened it. If the owner opens it on his or her phone and the office manager opens it on hers, they each get a separate copy and see different requests. Sitting side by side comparing screens, it looks broken.

Say it before they look, not after:

> "Anything you type into the demo stays on the device you typed it on, so you and your office will each see your own copy. A real install is one shared list."

---

## 2. The email

**Subject:** A service-request demo I built for Kennedy's

> Hello —
>
> My name is [YOUR NAME]. I write software, and I'm in [YOUR TOWN]. Nobody at Kennedy's asked me for this, so I'll say that first.
>
> On my own time I built a working demo of an online service-request system, and I built it around your shop. The customer picks the trade, then the actual problem, answers a few short follow-up questions, adds photos, and gives the days that work for them. Your office sees the request already sorted by urgency. If someone reports gas, smoke, or water they can't shut off, it stops and tells them to call 911.
>
> You can open it on your phone: [DEMO URL]
>
> Would you be open to ten minutes, by phone or at your office? If it's worth going further, I'll put a written price to it.
>
> Thanks for the time.
>
> [YOUR NAME]
> [YOUR PHONE]
> [YOUR EMAIL]

**Rules for this email:**
- One link. The demo. No portfolio, no scheduling page, no attachments.
- Plain text. No logo, no banner, no graphics in the signature. It should look like a person typed it.
- If you are not actually in or near Grant County, cut "and I'm in [YOUR TOWN]" rather than stretching it.
- Do not use the word "AI" anywhere in it.
- Do not name a price, a number of leads, or a response time. Kennedy's decides what they promise their customers.

---

## 3. The short version

Under 60 words. For a text, a Facebook page message, or the body of a voicemail follow-up email.

> Hi — my name is [YOUR NAME], I write software in [YOUR TOWN]. Nobody at Kennedy's asked me for this. On my own time I built a working online service-request demo around your shop: customer picks the problem, answers a few questions, sends photos, your office gets it sorted by urgency. [DEMO URL] — worth a look? [YOUR PHONE]

A Facebook message probably lands with whoever runs the page, not the owner. Close with: *"If this should go to someone else, point me at them and I'll take it from there."*

---

## 4. Voicemail script

About 20 seconds. Say the number twice, slower than feels natural.

> "Good morning. My name is [YOUR NAME] — this is for whoever handles the office side.
>
> Nobody at Kennedy's asked me for this. On my own time I built a working demo of an online service-request form, built around your shop. I'll email the link this morning.
>
> My number is [YOUR PHONE]. That's [YOUR PHONE]. Thank you."

Don't pitch on the machine. The job of the message is to make the email expected. No "I'll try you back later." Leave it once, never twice.

**If a person answers:** they are working. Keep it under fifteen seconds.

> "Morning — I'm not a customer and I won't keep you. My name is [YOUR NAME]. I built a small demo for Kennedy's on my own time, unasked. Who's the right person to email it to, and what's their name?"

Get the name, thank them, hang up.

## 5. Follow-up email (4–5 days later, no reply)

Under 80 words. One new fact. Reply in the same thread so the original sits underneath.

**Subject:** (reply in thread) — or, starting fresh: *One more thing on that Kennedy's demo*

> [NAME] —
>
> One thing I left out.
>
> The office side ranks every request and prints the reason for the ranking in plain English. It's a fixed set of rules, not a guess, so you can read the order and tell me where it's wrong.
>
> Same link: [DEMO URL]
>
> Still glad to walk you through it. No reply needed if it's not for you.
>
> [YOUR NAME]

Never open with "just following up," "circling back," or "bumping this." Open with the new fact.

---

## 6. Close-out (about a week later)

The last message you send.

**Subject:** (reply in thread) — *Closing this out*

> [NAME] —
>
> I'll stop here.
>
> I'll leave the demo up at [DEMO URL]. Nothing to sign up for, and it doesn't expire. If furnace season gets loud and you want another look, my number is [YOUR PHONE].
>
> Thanks for your time either way.
>
> [YOUR NAME]

Three messages and one voicemail is the whole campaign. Reopen it only if you have something genuinely new — a change they'd care about, or one of their customers asking for it.

## 7. If they reply

### A. "Sure, let's talk"

Answer inside the hour if you can. Don't send a longer email than the one that worked.

> Good — thank you. Would Thursday at 8:30 or Friday at 8:30 work? Either one, I'll keep it to ten minutes and I'll come to you if that's easier. Nothing you need to have ready. If someone else in the office answers the phone all day, it's worth having them there too. — [YOUR NAME]

Offer two times rather than open availability, and honor the ten minutes — if they want longer, they'll say so. Bring your phone with the intake already open, and read `sales/DEMO-SCRIPT.md` out loud beforehand.

### B. "How much?"

Don't dodge and don't quote blind. **Decide your numbers tonight, before you send anything.**

> Fair question. For the build as you see it in the demo, I'd expect around [PRICE RANGE] one time, plus about [HOSTING/MONTH] a month to host it — and I'd rather see how your office actually takes a call before I put a number in writing. A short call gets me that. You'll have a fixed written price within a few days, and if it's out of line you tell me so and we're done. — [YOUR NAME]

Never answer "how much" with "what's your budget." Don't volunteer anything about model vendors or API costs; if they ask directly, the true answer is that the system runs without a paid model vendor and that cost is not something they inherit.

### C. "We're happy with what we have"

Accept it in the first sentence. This is the reply most people lose by arguing.

> Understood — that's a good answer, and I won't push. The demo stays up at [DEMO URL] if you ever want it. If the office side ever gets to be too much, I'm at [YOUR PHONE]. Thanks for writing back. — [YOUR NAME]

Do not follow this with "before you go, can I just ask —".

### D. "Where does the request actually land?"

The first real question an owner asks, and the demo does not answer it on its own.

> In the demo, everything stays in the browser you opened it in. Built for real, every request lands in one shared list your office opens in a web browser — nothing to install — and the office gets an email as soon as one comes in, so nobody has to sit watching a screen. If your office would rather not learn another screen at all, the request can come to them as an email they read like any other. — [YOUR NAME]

Only say the parts you will actually build. Email notification is not in the demo. If you name it, it is scope you have agreed to.

### E. "Who's liable if that emergency screen tells my customer the wrong thing?"

The most likely objection to the safety interlock. Have this ready.

> Fair thing to ask before anything goes up under your name. The form never diagnoses equipment and never tells anyone a situation is safe. When someone reports gas, smoke, a CO alarm, or water they can't stop, it stops taking the request and tells them to get out and call 911 or the gas utility — the same thing your office would tell them on the phone. You own that wording: change it, shorten it, or take the screen out and I'll do it. Before it goes live under your name, I'd want you to run the wording past whoever handles your insurance. — [YOUR NAME]

Do not give legal opinions. Point at the wording, hand them control of it, and tell them to have it read.

### F. "You're one guy. What happens when you're not around?"

Do not wave this off. They have probably been burned by it already.

> Straight answer: I'm one person, and you should plan for me not being here. Three things make that survivable, and I'll put all three in writing. The code and your customer data live in accounts in Kennedy's name, not mine. It's built with ordinary tools that any competent developer can pick up — nothing homemade or locked. And you get a written handover document, in plain English, before you pay the last invoice. If I go quiet, you hand it to somebody else without needing my permission. — [YOUR NAME]

### G. "Who owns it if I stop paying you?"

> You do. The code and the data are yours from the first day, and you can take an export of every request at any time. Hosting is the only thing you'd keep paying for, and you can move that anywhere. — [YOUR NAME]

### H. No reply

Silence is not a no and it is not personal. Follow the sequence in Section 8, then stop.

## 8. Timing and channel

What is actually known: their office is open Monday to Friday, 8:00 to 5:00, and closed Saturday and Sunday. Everything else about their week is a guess, so don't build a plan on it.

- Send early in the morning, mid-week rather than Monday, so it's near the top of the stack when somebody sits down. That's preference, not research.
- Never leave a weekend voicemail. They're closed and it's stale by Monday.
- As for the season: go now because the demo is finished now. You don't know what their September looks like.

**Sequence:**

1. **Day 1 (Tue):** the email.
2. **Day 2 or 3:** one phone call. Voicemail if no answer. Never a second voicemail.
3. **Day 5 or 6:** the follow-up email (Section 5).
4. **About a week later:** the close-out (Section 6).
5. Then nothing.

**Email opens, phone follows:**

1. The published number is the number their customers call. A stranger holding it up is an interruption, and that's what you'll be remembered as.
2. The demo is the pitch and the demo is a link. A link does not survive a phone call.
3. Email lets them look at it on their own time, without a stranger waiting on a reaction.
4. The call's job is not to pitch. It's to get a name and make the email expected.

## 9. What not to say

| Don't say | Say instead |
| --- | --- |
| "We" / "our team" / "my agency" | "I." You are one person with low overhead. |
| "I work with a lot of HVAC companies" | "This is the first one I've built, and I built it for yours." |
| "It diagnoses the problem before the tech arrives" | "It never diagnoses anything. It collects, and it flags the safety stuff." |
| "It books the appointment automatically" | "It doesn't book anybody. It collects the request and your office still decides." |
| "This will get you 30% more bookings" | "It should save the office time on each call. You put your own numbers in and see." |
| "You need this" / "every modern business has this" | "Have a look and tell me whether it's useful. It might not be." |
| "Free trial, no risk, act now" | "It's a demo you can open on your phone." |
