# Kennedy's Smart Service Desk — Demo Script

For [YOUR NAME]. Read it out loud before you walk in.

**Runtime:** about two minutes of clicking if nobody stops you — call it ninety seconds if you skip the optional last beat. Assume you are in the building fifteen. If they ask a question, stop clicking and answer it.

**If you are sending the link instead of standing there**, the app has its own guided walkthrough at `/demo` — a bar at the bottom of the screen tells the owner what to click, one step at a time, and it can be dismissed. That is what the link in your email leads to.

**Three standing rules:**

1. Say nothing about their current website. This sits beside it.
2. You drive. Do not hand a stranger your phone and ask him to pretend he is a customer in his own lobby.
3. Do not claim anything about how their office runs. You have never been inside it. Where the old version of this script asserted, ask instead.

---

## Before you walk in

1. **Run both sides in one browser on the laptop.** Every browser keeps its own copy of the demo data. A request submitted on your phone will not appear on the laptop. Two tabs in the same browser: tab one the intake, window narrowed so it lays out like a phone; tab two the dashboard. Rehearse the switch until it is boring. Never refresh or retry in front of them.
2. **Fresh private window that morning.** Clean inbox. Nothing in it named "asdf".
3. **Customer details in autofill.** Name, phone, email, a real Marion street address, ZIP.
4. **One photo saved to the desktop.** A model-and-serial plate off a furnace or condenser. You upload it during the walkthrough.
5. **Laptop charged, notifications off, screen clean.**
6. **Know who you are talking to.** Office hours are 8 to 5, so the person at the counter is probably the office manager or a dispatcher.

---

## If the owner is not there

This is the most likely thing that happens. Do not ask for him, get told he is on a job, and walk back out.

> "That's fine. You'd use this more than he would anyway. Can I show you two minutes of it?"

Run the same walkthrough for whoever is standing there. Office staff will tell you what is wrong with it faster than an owner will. At the end, ask for the owner's name, the best day to come back, and leave the one-page proposal with the link on it.

## The walkthrough

### Beat 1 — Say why you are there

**CLICK:** Nothing. Laptop open, intake tab up, turned so they can see it.

**SAY:**

> "Before I start — nobody at Kennedy's asked me for this. I built it on my own time because I wanted to see if I could. Thirty seconds and you can tell me it's useless. A customer's air conditioning just quit."

**THEY SEE:** Six service categories. Big buttons, nothing to read.

---

### Beat 2 — Two taps to the real problem

**CLICK:** **Air Conditioning** → **Blowing warm air**.

**SAY:**

> "What kind of work, then what it's actually doing. The customer picks from a list instead of typing a paragraph into a message box."

**THEY SEE:** The AC issue list — Not cooling, Blowing warm air, Won't turn on, Unusual noise, Leaking or icing up, Tune-up, Replacement estimate. Each with a plain-English hint under it.

---

### Beat 3 — The follow-up questions

**CLICK:** Four questions, one tap each.

1. _What is the system doing right now?_ → **Running, but the air isn't cold**
2. _Is the outdoor unit running?_ → **Humming or buzzing, but the fan isn't turning**
3. _When did this start?_ → **Today**
4. _Roughly how old is the equipment?_ → **10 to 15 years**

**SAY:**

> "These are my guess at what your office asks before it sends a truck. The questions change depending on what they picked. Tell me what's missing or what's wrong — that's the part I need from you."

Then be quiet and let them answer. Write down what they say.

**THEY SEE:** Four short questions, tap-to-answer, one screen at a time. No typing yet.

---

### Beat 4 — The safety stop (show it, do not describe it)

**CLICK:** Back up one screen. Choose the answer that reports a gas smell. Let the red screen fill the screen and sit there for a second. Then back out and carry on.

**SAY:**

> "That's the one thing in here I'd defend. Gas smell, CO alarm, smoke or burning, sparking, flooding they can't shut off, sewage. The form stops. Get out, call 911 or the gas company. It will not take the request and it never tries to tell them what's wrong with the equipment."

Do not explain this screen twice. Showing it is the whole argument.

---

### Beat 5 — Urgency

**CLICK:** **Today if possible**.

**SAY:**

> "How soon they need somebody. Four choices, and it carries through to your inbox."

**THEY SEE:** Emergency, Today if possible, Next available, Planning ahead.

---

### Beat 6 — Who they are, the photo, and when they are home

**CLICK:** Autofill the contact fields. On the photo step, upload the model-plate photo you saved and let the preview come up. Pick two days on the calendar and choose **Afternoon**.

**SAY:**

> "Name, phone, address. They can add photos — the unit, the problem area, the model plate. That one's a model plate. Then which days they're actually home and what part of the day."

**THEY SEE:** A normal contact form, an optional photo step with three suggestions and a thumbnail preview, then a date picker with Morning / Afternoon / Evening.

Do not skip the photo. Every screen after this one shows it.

---

### Beat 7 — Review and send

**CLICK:** Scroll the summary. **Submit**. Switch to the dashboard tab.

**SAY:**

> "They see everything before it goes, then a reference number they can hold onto. That's the customer finished. Now your side."

**THEY SEE:** The full structured summary, then a confirmation with a reference number, a call button, and an edit option.

---

### Beat 8 — It is already at the top of the inbox

**SAY:**

> "There it is. Top of the list, ranked High, and it tells you why in words. Warm air, wanted today, system is ten-plus years old. That's a rule, not a guess. Same request, same rank, every time — no model deciding the order of your own work."

**THEY SEE:** Summary cards across the top — New, Needs response, Emergency, Scheduled, Estimate opportunities, Completed — and the new request at the top of the inbox with its rank and the reasons underneath.

---

### Beat 9 — The estimate flag

**CLICK:** Point at the estimate-opportunity flag on the card.

**SAY:**

> "It noticed the system is old. It doesn't recommend replacing anything and it doesn't tell you to sell. You decide."

---

### Beat 10 — The call summary

**CLICK:** Open the request. Point at the call summary at the top of the Smart Office Assist panel.

**SAY:**

> "This is what I'd want if I answered your phone. Two or three sentences to read before you dial. Everything they told us is underneath it in order, and the photo is right there."

**THEY SEE:** Customer info, the structured answers, urgency, preferred windows, the photo, the intake history, and a short written call summary.

---

### Beat 11 — The reply draft and the prep sheet (end here)

**CLICK:** Scroll the assist panel to the reply draft, then the technician prep sheet. Stop on the prep sheet.

**SAY:**

> "A reply already drafted for the customer, email or text. And a prep sheet for whoever takes the truck — reported issue, equipment, symptoms, rough age, the photo, anything flagged as a safety concern, and what to verify onsite. Nothing sends by itself. Those are copy buttons. A person still hits send."

> "One more thing before you ask me: right now this is a demo with no login on it. Names, phone numbers, addresses, photos of the inside of somebody's house. A real install gets logins and the data lives in a proper database on your account instead of in a browser. I'd rather tell you that than have you find it."

**THEY SEE:** A customer reply draft with a copy button, and the technician prep sheet below it.

---

### Beat 12 — The one that never finished (only if they are still with you)

Skip this if they have checked out. If they are leaning in, it is the beat that answers the objection they have not said out loud yet.

**CLICK:** Back to the inbox. Find the request marked **Unfinished** — Gail Sandiford — and open it.

**SAY:**

> "Here's the one I'd want you to look at. She never finished. Got as far as putting her name and number in and closed it. You still have her: name, number, address, thermostat's dead, breaker tripped, wants somebody today. On a contact form that request doesn't exist — she just never shows up."

**THEY SEE:** A yellow banner saying the request was never finished, what is missing (photos and preferred times), and everything she had already entered.

Say nothing after that. Let them work out what it is worth.

---

**Then stop.** Hands off the keyboard.

If they are still with you, two things you can point at without clicking: the status pipeline — New, Contacted, Scheduled, Technician assigned, Estimate sent, Completed — cards moved by dragging, and a Business Impact page with a time calculator. That page is arithmetic, not a promise, and they put in their own numbers. Leave it for them to open on their own link rather than asking a man you met four minutes ago to guess how many calls a week his office takes.

## If they interrupt

One or two sentences, then back to clicking.

**"Who sees this?"**

> "Your office and you. The customer only ever sees the form and their own confirmation. As it stands it's a demo with no login — logins and a real database are the first thing in a paid install."

**"We're closed nights and weekends. Somebody fills this out Saturday at nine — then what?"**

> "It lands in the inbox, timestamped and ranked, and it's there Monday at eight sorted instead of a row of voicemails. Nobody gets woken up unless you set up a notification, and I'm not going to promise a customer a response time you haven't promised them. If it's actually dangerous, the form never takes the request — it tells them to get out and call 911 or the gas company."

**"We've already got somebody who does our website."**

> "Good, keep them. This isn't a website and I'm not touching theirs. It's one link off your contact page, or off nothing at all. I'll work with whoever they are."

**"Does it connect to our scheduling?"**

> "Not today. It's built so it can, and that's a defined piece of work rather than a rewrite. Tell me what you run for dispatch and I'll tell you straight whether it connects and what's involved."

**"What if somebody's actually in danger?"**

> Point at the red screen again instead of describing it. You already showed it.

**"Does it cost per use?"**

> "No. What you just watched runs with no AI service behind it — those summaries are written from the answers the customer gave. There's a version that can call a paid service if you ever want it, and it falls back to this one if that service is down. You'd never be stuck paying by the request just to use it."

**"You're one guy. What happens to us in three years?"**

> "Fair question and you should ask it first. The source code is yours, in writing, day one. It's built in standard tools that any web developer can pick up, and I'll hand over the code and written setup notes whether or not I'm still around. Nothing in it is locked to me and there's no monthly fee that switches it off."

**"Could my office manager actually use this?"**

> "That's the real test, and I'd want them to try it instead of you. I'd sit with them and I'd rather they tell me what's wrong with it than be polite about it."

**"What would this cost us?"**

> Give the range off your pricing sheet. Once. Then: "I'll put a written quote in your hands with a fixed number and a date on it this week."

## What to say at the end

Close the laptop halfway. Stop clicking.

> "That's the whole thing. You didn't ask for it and I'm not going to stand here and tell you it's right for Kennedy's. Give me ten minutes this week — you or whoever answers the phone — and tell me what's wrong with it."

Then be quiet and let them answer.

If they say yes: get a day and a time before you leave the building. Not "sometime next week."

---

## What not to do

1. **Don't over-explain.** You built every corner of it and you want to show all of it. They don't need the architecture. One sentence per question, then back to clicking. When they are reading a screen, stay quiet.

2. **Don't apologize for it being a concept.** Say once, at the top, that nobody asked you for it. That's honesty and it buys you credit. Saying it three more times turns it into an apology.

3. **Don't promise a timeline you can't hit.** "Couple of weeks" said casually becomes a hard date in their head, and missing it is the first thing they'll remember about you. Say: "I'd want to see what you're running before I give you a date. When I give you one, I'll hit it."

4. **Don't make them ask for a price twice.** First time they ask, give the range and a date for the written quote, and go back to the demo. Making a busy owner ask three times gets you shown the door.

5. **Don't state anything about their operation as fact.** You have not worked a day in their office. Everything you think you know about their intake is a question you ask them.
